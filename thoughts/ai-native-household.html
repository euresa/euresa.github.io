Towards an AI-Native Household

October 2026

Most of my attention at work this year has gone toward figuring out how to build software with AI, and at some point it started to feel a bit odd that none of it was making it home. My wife and I deal with the same pile of stuff every household does: email, paperwork, shared documents, appointments, and a long list of small things nobody ever writes down. We'd like to bring AI into our household the way I've been bringing it into my work. I also think that getting good at working with AI systems is going to matter for basically everyone over the next few years, and the best way I know to get better at something is to use it for real things every day.

The first thing we needed was a good memory. We've put a lot of work into building a household "vault," a second brain built on Obsidian and the LLM wiki pattern that Andrej Karpathy outlined. The idea is that instead of having an AI re-read a pile of raw documents every time you ask it something, you have it build and maintain a wiki of linked markdown pages that gets better over time. To get ours started, we had agents go through our past text messages and Google Voice conversations, our email, and the documents we had scattered across Google Docs and Notion, and distill all of it into a set of pages about us. It took more work than I expected, mostly deciding what belonged and what didn't, but we now have a source of truth that both of us (and our agents) can read.

On top of the vault is the assistant itself. We use Hermes, an open source agent from Nous Research, running on a small DigitalOcean server so that it keeps working when my laptop is closed. We talk to it through Discord, which turned out to be a great interface because it's already on both of our phones and feels a lot like texting a very capable assistant.

Hermes can work with our Gmail, Google Docs and Sheets, and Notion, and it has a read-only copy of the vault that syncs from an encrypted backup on GitHub. For models it goes through OpenRouter, and we mostly use very cheap ones, since most of what we ask isn't that hard. When something needs real research, Hermes has an instruction to hand it off to Claude Code and report back.

So far it's working pretty well. We did think about getting dedicated hardware for it, either a Mac mini or something like an NVIDIA Jetson Orin, but we expect to experiment and change things quickly over the next few months. Spending money up front on hardware doesn't make much sense while everything is moving this fast, and a small cloud server is cheap and easy to throw away. If we ever get more concerned about privacy or how much we trust the model providers, hosting our own open-weight models is probably where we'd go next, and that's when the hardware would start to make sense.
